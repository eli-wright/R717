      01 Module 01 · Overview

      Why ammonia still runs the cold chain

      Ammonia (R-717) has been used in industrial refrigeration since the 1870s. It is still the first choice for cold storage, food processing and large process cooling. This course explains how these systems work and how to work on them safely.

      Why ammonia?

      • Zero ozone depletion and zero global warming potential. ODP 0 and GWP 0.
      • Very high latent heat. About 564 BTU/lb at 5°F. Each pound that boils soaks up a lot of heat, so less refrigerant has to flow.
      • Lower running cost. Smaller pipes and lower energy use than most synthetic refrigerants in large plants.
      • Easy to find and cheap. It is made in huge volumes for fertilizer.
      • The trade-off: it is toxic, and it can burn in a narrow range. That is why codes, gas detection, ventilation and training matter so much.

      02 Module 02 · The refrigeration cycle

      The vapor-compression cycle

      Refrigeration moves heat from where you don't want it to where it does no harm. The refrigerant does this by boiling at low pressure and condensing at high pressure, over and over, in a closed loop.

      Liquid Liquid + vapor Vapor Hot gas
      The whole loop

      Four processes, one closed loop

      Low-pressure vapor is compressed, the hot gas is condensed to liquid, the liquid is expanded through a valve, and the cold liquid evaporates as it absorbs heat. Then it starts again.

      Choose a component on the diagram, or a step above, to zoom in.

      Key ideas

      Saturation, superheat and subcooling

      Saturation

      Liquid and vapor together, at the boiling point. In this state pressure sets temperature. At 151.7 psig, ammonia boils or condenses at 85°F. At 12.2 psig, it boils at −5°F.

      Superheat

      Heat added to vapor after it has fully boiled. A direct-expansion (DX) coil uses a few degrees of superheat to make sure only vapor leaves the coil.

      Superheat = Tactual − Tsaturation

      Subcooling

      Heat removed from liquid after it has fully condensed. Subcooled liquid makes less flash gas at the expansion valve, so each pound circulated gives more refrigeration effect.

      Subcooling = Tsaturation − Tactual
      Good to know

      Ammonia facts

      You can smell it early

      Most people smell ammonia at about 5 to 50 ppm, far below the 300 ppm IDLH. Your nose gets used to it, though, so never rely on smell. Rely on detectors.

      Lighter than air — usually

      Ammonia gas is lighter than air (molecular weight 17 vs 29 for air), so a warm vapor leak rises. But a release of cold liquid makes a dense fog of tiny droplets (an aerosol) that is heavier than air and can stay low to the ground. Do not assume a leak will always rise.

      It is natural

      Ammonia occurs in nature and breaks down quickly in the environment. Unlike synthetic refrigerants, it does not linger in the atmosphere.

      Same cycle, different scales

      From a freezer warehouse to your kitchen

      The four-step cycle is universal. Only the size, the parts and the refrigerant change.

      Industrial ammonia plant

      Evaporation in large air units in a freezer warehouse, or in a plate chiller cooling glycol. Compression by screw compressors. Condensation in evaporative condensers on the roof. Expansion through industrial control valves.

      Home air conditioner or heat pump

      Evaporation in the indoor coil in the air handler. Compression and condensation in the outdoor unit. Expansion through a small TXV or orifice. A heat pump adds a reversing valve that swaps the jobs of the indoor and outdoor coils.

      Kitchen refrigerator

      Evaporation in the coils in the freezer. Compression in the small sealed compressor at the back. Condensation in the coils on the back or underneath, which warm your kitchen. Expansion through a thin capillary tube.

      R-717 · P-h diagram
      Decoding the diagram

      The three states of ammonia

      Subcooled liquid (left)

      Stable liquid. At this pressure it is colder than its boiling point, so it will not boil. Subcooled liquid going to an expansion valve makes less flash gas.

      The two-phase dome (center)

      Inside the dome, the refrigerant is saturated: boiling liquid and vapor together. Here pressure and temperature are locked together — one pressure, one temperature. This is where it absorbs or rejects large amounts of heat without changing temperature. The dotted lines show the vapor share by weight (quality).

      Superheated vapor (right)

      Stable gas, hotter than its boiling point at this pressure. Compressor discharge gas is strongly superheated, and the condenser must remove that heat first. The orange lines are lines of constant temperature.

      A refrigerant's journey

      Mapping the parts to the diagram

      Evaporation: the heat sponge

      In the evaporator, the refrigerant soaks up heat at a nearly constant low pressure and boils at a nearly constant low temperature. On the chart this is the horizontal line along the bottom, moving right as enthalpy rises.

      Compression: the big squeeze

      The compressor takes in vapor and adds work. Pressure and temperature rise sharply. An accumulator ahead of it keeps liquid out. On the chart this line climbs up and to the right.

      Condensation: the cool-down

      In the condenser the hot gas first desuperheats — its temperature drops at constant pressure. Then it condenses at constant temperature. Then the liquid may subcool a little. It drains to the high-pressure receiver. On the chart this is the horizontal line along the top, moving left.

      Expansion: the pressure plunge

      At the expansion valve the pressure drops in an instant with no change in enthalpy, so the chart shows a straight vertical line down. The lower pressure means a lower boiling point, so part of the liquid flashes and the rest is chilled.

      Advanced cycles

      Economizers and intercoolers

      Economizer

      An economizer flashes part of the liquid at an intermediate pressure. That cools (subcools) the rest of the liquid, so less flash gas forms at the evaporator's valve. On a screw compressor, the flash vapor goes to a side port partway through compression, where it takes less energy to compress. On the chart, the liquid line shifts left before expansion.

      Intercooler (two-stage systems)

      Two-stage systems typically use an intercooler between the low-stage (booster) and high-stage compressors. It desuperheats the booster discharge gas, which saves high-stage power, and it usually subcools the liquid going to the low-temperature evaporators too. On the chart you see a third, intermediate pressure level.

      Deeper dive

      The “why” behind the diagram

      States of matter: a pot of water

      • Subcooled liquid: 70°F water at sea level. It boils at 212°F, so it is a stable liquid.
      • Saturated: water at 212°F, boiling, with water and steam together. Add heat and you make more steam, but the temperature will not rise until the water is gone. This is what happens in an evaporator and a condenser.
      • Superheated vapor: all the water is gone and you keep heating the steam. It gets hotter than its boiling point. This is what a compressor takes in and puts out.

      Why pressure stays nearly constant in the evaporator and condenser

      Inside these coils the refrigerant is saturated, so pressure and temperature are locked together and heat goes into changing phase. The pressure stays steady because it is set by a balance:

      • Evaporator: heat coming in from the load versus how fast the compressor pumps vapor away. More load than the compressor can handle and suction pressure rises.
      • Condenser: heat the condenser can reject versus the heat arriving from the compressor. If the condenser can't keep up, condensing pressure rises.

      Flow through tubes and pipes also causes small friction pressure drops, so “constant” really means “nearly constant.”

      Why compression raises P and T, and expansion lowers them

      • Compression: the compressor squeezes vapor into a smaller space. Like pumping up a bike tire, the work raises both pressure and temperature.
      • Expansion: the valve is a restriction. High-pressure liquid is forced through it into a low-pressure space. The pressure drops at once, and because boiling point follows pressure, the temperature drops too.

      Pressure difference: the garden hose

      Put your thumb over a garden hose and the water speeds up. You made a restriction; the water main did not get stronger. Flow, restriction and the source work together.

      • The compressor creates the pressure difference between the low side and the high side.
      • The condensing pressure is set by how well the condenser rejects heat: the outdoor temperature (the wet-bulb temperature for an evaporative condenser), clean or fouled surfaces, working fans and pumps, and any air trapped in the system.
      • A recirculator pump adds only a small pressure rise — enough to overcome pipe friction and lift and push liquid through the evaporators. The low-side pressure itself is set by the compressors pulling vapor out of the vessel, balanced against the load.

      How the vessels are built

      • Material: carbon steel (impact tested where the low temperature calls for it) or stainless steel. Never copper or brass.
      • Shape: a cylindrical shell with formed heads; 2:1 semi-elliptical heads are common.
      • Code: built and stamped to the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, Section VIII, Division 1.
      • Function: set by the nozzles, the internals and where the vessel sits in the system.
      • Orientation: vertical or horizontal.

      Design philosophy

      Each vessel has one main job: store liquid, separate liquid from vapor, or hold a pressure level. Keeping liquid out of compressors and keeping oil where it can be drained are the two rules that shape most of the system.

      Liquid recirculation

      Inside a recirculator: overfeed in motion

      Reading a 3:1 overfeed ratio

      • For every 3 lb of liquid pumped to the evaporator…
      • 1 lb boils, absorbing heat and doing the cooling.
      • 2 lb stay liquid and ride back with the vapor. This is the “wet return.”

      Overfeed keeps the whole inside surface of the coil wet, which gives strong heat transfer. The vessel then separates the liquid (to pump again) from the dry vapor (to the compressors).

      Where the oil goes

      Ammonia oil does not mix with ammonia. It is heavier, so the little that gets past the oil separators sinks and collects in low-side vessels — recirculators, surge drums and accumulators — and at low points. It is drained from oil pots on a schedule using a safe procedure (see Module 07).

      05 Module 05 · System architectures

      Three ways to feed an evaporator

      How liquid gets to the evaporators defines the whole system. Each method has a place.

      Direct expansion (DX)

      An expansion valve meters just enough liquid to boil off completely, with a few degrees of superheat at the coil outlet.

      Advantages

      • Simple
      • Smallest refrigerant charge
      • Lowest first cost

      Drawbacks

      • The last part of the coil only superheats vapor, so heat transfer is lower
      • Most common in smaller and low-charge systems
      Compressor→Oil separator→Condenser→HPR→DX valve→Evaporator→Accumulator→Compressor

      Flooded

      A surge drum keeps the evaporator full of liquid by gravity. A float or level control feeds liquid to the drum.

      Advantages

      • Fully wetted surface, strong heat transfer
      • Stable operation

      Drawbacks

      • Larger charge
      • Oil collects in the drum and evaporator and must be drained
      • Needs good level control
      Compressor→Oil separator→Condenser→HPR→Level control→Surge drum ⇄ Evaporator→Dry suction→Compressor

      Liquid recirculation

      A recirculator package pumps 2 to 4 times the liquid that boils from a central vessel to many evaporators.

      Advantages

      • Fully wetted coils, high heat transfer
      • Many evaporators served from one central vessel
      • Simple controls at each evaporator
      • Most common in large plants

      Drawbacks

      • Higher first cost
      • Pumps to maintain
      • Larger charge
      Compressor→Oil separator→Condenser→HPR or CPR→Liquid make-up→Recirculator + pumps→Evaporators→Wet return→Dry suction

      A two-stage version adds an intercooler between the booster and high-stage compressors.

      System selection guide

      What usually fits?

      Large, many evaporators: liquid recirculation

      For big multi-room plants — food processing, cold storage warehouses — recirculation is the usual choice. One central vessel and pump set serves many evaporators with fully wetted coils, which justifies the higher first cost.

      These are common starting points, not rules. Charge limits, codes, load profile and owner preference all shape the final choice.

      06 Module 06 · The science of flash gas

      Flash gas: the price of getting cold

      Flash gas is the vapor that forms when high-pressure liquid suddenly drops in pressure. It is not a flaw — it is physics. Whether it helps or hurts depends on where it happens.

      Needed: right after the expansion valve

      Flash gas that forms just after the valve is doing its job. That instant boiling chills the rest of the liquid down to evaporator temperature.

      A problem: before the valve

      Flash gas in the liquid line, before the valve, is always a problem. The vapor takes up space, cuts capacity and makes valves misbehave. Look for too much pressure drop, a liquid line that rises too far, heat gain, or low charge.

      An analogy: water on a mountain

      Water boils at 212°F at sea level. At 10,000 feet the air pressure is lower, and water boils at about 194°F. The water didn't get hotter; the lower pressure lowered its boiling point.

      Refrigerant does the same thing. Warm, high-pressure liquid goes through the expansion valve into low pressure. Its boiling point drops at once, so the liquid is suddenly hotter than its new boiling point. The only quick way to cool down is for part of it to boil. That part is the flash gas.

      The physics: an energy balance

      1. Before the valve: warm, high-pressure liquid. High pressure keeps it from boiling.
      2. Through the valve: no heat added or removed and no work done, so the enthalpy stays the same.
      3. After the valve: the same energy, but at a pressure where it's too much energy to stay all liquid.
      4. The fix: a fraction boils. Boiling takes a lot of energy (latent heat), pulled from the remaining liquid, which cools to evaporator temperature.
      Worked example

      Ammonia from 85°F liquid to a −5°F evaporator

      % flash gas = (h − hf) ÷ hfg × 100
      • h — enthalpy of the liquid before the valve. The energy budget.
      • hf — enthalpy of saturated liquid at the low pressure. The most energy it can keep and stay all liquid.
      • h − hf — the extra energy that must go.
      • hfg — the energy to boil one pound at the low pressure.

      So the formula is just extra energy ÷ cost of boiling.

      Scenario: saturated liquid ammonia at 85°F (151.7 psig) is expanded to −5°F (12.2 psig).

      1. Starting energy

        Saturated liquid at 85°F: h = 137.6 BTU/lb.

      2. Properties at −5°F

        hf = 37.5 BTU/lb and hg = 609.5 BTU/lb.

      3. Cost of boiling

        hfg = 609.5 − 37.5 = 572.0 BTU/lb.

      4. Do the math

        (137.6 − 37.5) ÷ 572.0 = 17.5%

      Result: 17.5% of the mass turns to flash gas before it cools anything. For every 100 lb that passes the valve, about 82.5 lb of liquid is left to do the cooling. By volume, the flash vapor fills about 99% of the pipe right after the valve.

      Subcooling

      What the “subcooled” box means

      When to use it: tick the box when the liquid going to the valve is colder than its saturation temperature. Enter how many degrees colder it is.

      Example: at 151.7 psig, ammonia is saturated at 85°F. With 3.6°F of subcooling the liquid is at 81.4°F. Flash gas to −5°F drops from 17.5% to 16.8%. With 10°F of subcooling it drops to 15.5%. Each degree helps a little: less flash gas and more refrigeration effect per pound.

      Where subcooling comes from

      • In the condenser: on a cool day, or with extra condenser surface, the liquid can cool a few degrees below saturation before it leaves.
      • A dedicated subcooler: a heat exchanger whose only job is to cool the high-pressure liquid.
      • Economizers and shell-and-coil intercoolers: they use liquid boiling at an intermediate pressure to subcool the main liquid.
      • Liquid-suction heat exchangers: common with halocarbon refrigerants, but rarely used with ammonia, because the added suction superheat raises ammonia's already high discharge temperature.

      Critical procedure

      Draining oil from an oil pot

      Oil collects in low-side vessels and must be removed. Opening anything to atmosphere on an ammonia system is a high-risk task. This is a general outline.

      Your site's written procedure (PSM) always governs. Follow it step by step. If it differs from this outline, the site procedure wins.

      1. Prepare

        Wear PPE: face shield over goggles, ammonia-rated gloves and protective clothing. Keep a respirator on hand. Have a second trained person present. Know your exit and the nearest eyewash and shower.

      2. Isolate the oil pot

        Close the valve(s) between the vessel and the oil pot.

      3. Vent the pot to the low side

        Equalize or vent the pot back to suction — never to atmosphere.

      4. Let the ammonia boil off

        Ammonia trapped in the oil boils away to suction. Warm the pot if needed. Wait until the pot pressure is near zero and holding steady.

      5. Drain in short bursts

        Open the shut-off valve, then drain through the self-closing valve in short bursts. Stay at the valve the whole time. Never leave it unattended. Never tie, wedge or block it open.

      6. Close up

        Close both drain valves and cap the drain piping. Return the pot to service as your procedure says.

      7. Record it

        Write down how much oil came out, from which vessel, and when. Comparing oil drained with oil added shows how much is carrying over.

      8. Dispose of the oil

        Handle and dispose of the used oil per your site rules.

      ANSI/IIAR 2-2021, 5.9.3

      What the drain must have

      • A shut-off valve in series with a self-closing (spring-return) shut-off valve.
      • Drain piping that opens to atmosphere must be capped when not in use.

      A rigid-piped oil return or transfer system is another allowed method.

      Never

      • Vent the pot or drain line to atmosphere to get rid of pressure.
      • Leave an open drain unattended, or hold a self-closing valve open with anything.
      • Drain without a second trained person nearby.
      If there is a leak

      Emergency response

      1. Evacuate the area right away. Move upwind.
      2. Alert your emergency response team and follow your emergency response plan.
      3. Shut down equipment with the emergency shutdown switch outside the machinery room, if your plan calls for it and it is safe.
      4. Isolate refrigerant sources only if you are trained and equipped to do so.
      5. Never enter a leak area without proper PPE and SCBA. Only trained responders go in.

      Using water

      A water fog or spray on the vapor cloud absorbs ammonia and can knock it down.

      Never spray water directly onto a pool of liquid ammonia. It makes the liquid boil off faster and makes the vapor cloud bigger.

      For skin or eye contact, flush with plenty of water right away and get medical help.

      This module is training, not a substitute for the standards, your site's written procedures or your emergency response plan.

      08 Module 08 · Calculations and sizing

      From tons to pump GPM and vessel size

      The core equations behind a recirculated system, with a live calculator. Every property comes from the same CoolProp tables used across this site.

      Define the load

      One ton of refrigeration (TR) is the heat to melt 2,000 lb of ice in 24 hours: 12,000 BTU/h, or 200 BTU/min.

      Q (BTU/min) = TR × 200

      Mass flow that must boil

      Divide the load by the energy each pound picks up.

      m (lb/min) = TR × 200 ÷ (hvapor leaving − hliquid entering)

      In the coils, pumped liquid enters saturated at evaporator temperature, so the bottom is hg − hf = hfg. For the vapor leaving the vessel to the compressors, the liquid entering is the warmer make-up liquid, so that flow also includes its flash gas.

      Pumped mass (overfeed)

      The pumps send more liquid than boils.

      mpumped = N × mevaporated

      N is the overfeed ratio, commonly 2 to 4.

      Liquid flow for the pump

      GPM = (mpumped ÷ ρL) × 7.48

      ρL is liquid density in lb/ft³. There are 7.48 gallons in a cubic foot.

      Suction vapor flow

      ACFM = msuction × vg

      vg is the specific volume of saturated vapor (ft³/lb). Ammonia vapor at low temperature takes up a lot of room.

      Vapor speed limit (Souders–Brown)

      vmax = K × √((ρL − ρV) ÷ ρV)

      Above this speed, rising vapor carries droplets with it. K (ft/s) depends on vessel orientation and internals: horizontal vessels and vessels with mist eliminators can use higher values than a plain vertical vessel. Use the vessel maker's data or your design basis.

      Vapor flow area

      A (ft²) = ACFM ÷ (60 × vmax)

      For a vertical vessel, the minimum inside diameter is D = √(4A ÷ π). In a horizontal vessel, the vapor space above the liquid must give this area.

      09 Module 09 · Troubleshooting

      Find the cause, then fix it

      Start with what you can measure: pressures, temperatures, levels and amps. Compare them with the saturation tables and the P-h diagram. Search or filter the common problems below.

      High discharge / condensing pressure

      Symptoms

      • Higher compressor power.
      • Less cooling capacity.
      • Compressor may trip on the high-pressure cutout.

      Possible causes

      • Fouled condenser coil or tubes, or scale on an evaporative condenser.
      • Failed condenser fan or water pump.
      • Air or other non-condensables in the system.
      • Not enough air or water flow, or a high wet-bulb day.
      • Too much charge backing liquid up into the condenser.

      Solutions

      • Clean condenser surfaces and treat the water.
      • Repair or replace failed fans or pumps.
      • Purge non-condensables with an automatic purger.
      • Clear anything blocking air or water flow.
      • Safely remove excess charge to the right level.

      Low suction / evaporating pressure

      Symptoms

      • Less cooling in the room or process.
      • Compressor short-cycles on the low-pressure cutout.
      • Coils may frost up fast.

      Possible causes

      • Light load on the evaporator.
      • Clogged strainer in the liquid or suction line.
      • Heavy frost or ice blocking airflow.
      • Liquid solenoid or expansion valve stuck or closed.
      • Low refrigerant charge.

      Solutions

      • Check the load; a lightly loaded room runs lower.
      • Isolate, pump out and clean strainers.
      • Run a defrost and check it works.
      • Repair or replace faulty valves.
      • Find and fix leaks, then charge to the right level.

      Liquid carryover (slugging) to the compressor

      Symptoms

      • Loud knocking from the compressor.
      • Heavy frost on the suction line or (reciprocating) cylinder heads.
      • Unsteady oil pressure; trips on oil safety.
      • This can wreck a compressor in seconds.

      Possible causes

      • Expansion valve failed open or oversized, flooding the coil.
      • High liquid level in the accumulator or recirculator.
      • A sudden load change sending a surge of liquid back.
      • A badly set up or failed hot gas defrost.

      Solutions

      • Shut down the affected compressor right away.
      • Close the liquid feed to the problem evaporator.
      • Find the source: check vessel levels and level controls, and inspect valves.
      • After the fix, restart carefully and watch suction conditions.

      High discharge temperature

      Symptoms

      • Discharge temperature above normal. For reciprocating ammonia compressors, roughly 250–300°F (121–149°C) is the warning range.
      • Oil-injected screw compressors run much cooler, because the oil carries most of the heat of compression away. A high reading on a screw points first at the oil cooling.
      • Oil darkens or smells burnt.

      Possible causes

      • High compression ratio (low suction with high discharge pressure).
      • Too much suction superheat.
      • Reciprocating: poor head or jacket cooling; worn valves or rings.
      • Screw: oil cooler fouled, thermosyphon or liquid injection not working, low oil flow.

      Solutions

      • Fix low suction or high discharge pressure first.
      • Keep suction superheat low.
      • Check head cooling (reciprocating) or oil cooling and injection (screw).
      • If all else checks out, inspect the compressor internally.

      High power use

      Symptoms

      • Energy bills up without more load.
      • Compressor motor amps higher than normal.

      Possible causes

      • High condensing pressure (dirty condenser, non-condensables).
      • Low suction pressure, which raises the compression ratio.
      • Worn compressor parts.
      • Oil fouling heat exchanger surfaces.

      Solutions

      • Check the whole system against design pressures.
      • Clean condensers and evaporators.
      • Purge non-condensables.
      • Drain oil from low-side vessels on schedule; keep up oil changes and filters.

      Pump pressure and rotation problems

      Symptoms

      • Low or no pressure rise across the pump.
      • Very high pressure rise (dead-heading).
      • High amps with low flow.
      • Noise or vibration.

      Possible causes

      • Wrong rotation (low pressure): wired to run backwards.
      • Bypass open (low pressure): a relief or bypass valve stuck open.
      • Discharge closed (high pressure): a downstream valve shut.

      Solutions

      • Wrong rotation: lock out the pump and swap any two of the three motor leads.
      • Open bypass: check and close bypass and relief lines.
      • Closed discharge: stop the pump at once and open the required valves before restarting.

      Oil carryover and oil loss

      Symptoms

      • You keep adding oil to the compressors.
      • Low oil level or low-oil trips.
      • Oil building up in recirculators, surge drums and accumulators.
      • Evaporator capacity falling as oil coats surfaces.

      Possible causes

      • Oil separator not working well: worn or flooded coalescing elements, or a high oil level.
      • Running far from the separator's design point (for example, very low discharge pressure raises gas velocity).
      • Oil foaming from liquid returning to the compressor.
      • Oil pots not being drained, so oil spreads further into the low side.

      Solutions

      • Remember: ammonia oil does not travel back with the ammonia. It collects in the low side and must be drained.
      • Inspect and service the oil separator and its elements.
      • Drain oil pots on schedule using the safe procedure (Module 07).
      • Log oil added and oil drained to track carryover.

      Non-condensables (air) in the system

      Symptoms

      • Condensing pressure is higher than the saturation pressure at the temperature of the liquid leaving the condenser. Example: liquid leaving at 85°F should read about 151.7 psig. A noticeably higher gauge reading points to air.
      • High compressor power.

      Possible causes

      • Air leaking in where the low side runs below atmospheric pressure (below about −28°F for ammonia).
      • Poor evacuation after maintenance or a line opening.

      Solutions

      • Use an automatic non-condensable purger connected at the condenser outlets and the receiver.
      • Manual purging releases ammonia; do it only per your written procedure.
      • Find and fix the source of the leak.

      Refrigerant pump cavitation

      Symptoms

      • Rattling, gravel-like noise from the pump.
      • Pump pressure and flow jumping around.
      • Early wear of bearings.

      Possible causes

      • Not enough NPSH. The liquid in the vessel is saturated, so the only margin against flashing at the pump inlet is the static liquid head above the pump, minus losses in the pump suction line. A low vessel level means too little head.
      • Pump suction line too small, too long or with too many fittings.
      • Heat gain into the pump suction line.
      • A sudden drop in vessel pressure (for example, a compressor loading up fast) makes the liquid flash.
      • Clogged suction strainer.

      Solutions

      • Keep the right liquid level in the recirculator or surge drum.
      • Keep pump suction lines short, full size and well insulated.
      • Avoid fast swings in suction pressure.
      • Isolate, pump out and clean the suction strainer.

      Evaporator coil icing

      Symptoms

      • A solid block of ice on the coil.
      • Airflow badly restricted.
      • Room temperature rising.

      Possible causes

      • Defrost not working (controls, hot gas valves, drain heaters).
      • Too much moisture coming in (doors left open, poor seals).
      • Fans not stopping during defrost.

      Solutions

      • Troubleshoot the defrost system: timers, controls and valves.
      • Repair door seals and cut traffic into the room.
      • Make sure fans shut off during defrost.

      Solenoid valve failure

      Symptoms

      • Fails to open: no flow to an evaporator.
      • Fails to close: constant flow and a risk of liquid carryover.
      • Humming or buzzing coil.

      Possible causes

      • Burned-out coil.
      • Debris stopping the piston or diaphragm from seating.
      • Damaged internal parts.
      • Wrong voltage at the coil.

      Solutions

      • De-energize and check the coil; replace if needed.
      • Pump out the section, open the valve and clean it.
      • Use a repair kit for internal parts.
      • Check that supply voltage matches the coil rating.

      Pressure relief valve venting

      Symptoms

      • Sound of gas escaping.
      • Loss of refrigerant.
      • Ammonia detectors alarm.

      Possible causes

      • Overpressure from an outside heat source such as a fire.
      • Hydrostatic expansion: liquid trapped between closed valves warming up.
      • A control failure over-pressurizing the system.
      • A faulty or leaking relief valve.

      Solutions

      • Treat it as an active leak and follow your emergency plan.
      • Find and correct the cause of the overpressure.
      • Never block a relief valve or its discharge.
      • Replace a relief valve after it has discharged; it may not reseat tightly.

      Compressor short-cycling

      Symptoms

      • Starts and stops many times an hour.
      • Extra wear on starters and the compressor.

      Possible causes

      • Control setpoints too close together.
      • Compressor too big for the present load.
      • Faulty or badly placed sensors.
      • Fast changes in load.

      Solutions

      • Widen the control differential.
      • Use capacity control or a smaller machine for light loads.
      • Check sensor calibration and placement.
      • Smooth out load swings where you can.

      Chiller freeze-up

      Symptoms

      • Process fluid (glycol, brine, water) freezes in the chiller.
      • Low suction pressure and temperature.
      • Risk of ruptured plates or tubes and an ammonia release.

      Possible causes

      • Too little process fluid flow.
      • Low-temperature safety set too low or failed.
      • Refrigerant control valve misbehaving.
      • Wrong antifreeze concentration.

      Solutions

      • Check process pumps, valves and strainers.
      • Test and calibrate safeties to the maker's settings.
      • Repair refrigerant control valves.
      • Test the fluid's freeze point and adjust.

      High suction pressure

      Symptoms

      • Higher compressor load and power.
      • Room warmer than wanted.
      • Less dehumidification.

      Possible causes

      • Big load (lots of warm product brought in).
      • Not enough compressor capacity running.
      • Leaking hot gas or bypass valves.
      • Wrong pressure regulator settings.
      • DX: expansion valve stuck open or a loose sensing bulb.

      Solutions

      • Compare the load with design capacity.
      • Check compressor capacity control and staging.
      • Repair leaking valves.
      • Check and adjust regulator setpoints; check DX valves and bulbs.

      10 Module 10 · Questions and answers

      Common questions

      Short, plain answers to the questions people ask most.

      What is latent heat, and why is it the secret to refrigeration?

      Latent heat is the energy a substance takes in or gives off to change state — liquid to gas, for example — without changing temperature. Sensible heat is the kind you can see on a thermometer.

      Heat a pot of water from 70°F to 211°F and its temperature climbs: that is sensible heat. At 212°F it boils. The stove keeps pumping in heat, but the temperature stays at 212°F until the water is gone. That “hidden” energy is the latent heat of vaporization.

      An evaporator works the same way. Liquid ammonia at 5°F boils as it takes heat from the room, and stays at 5°F while it does. Ammonia absorbs about 564 BTU per pound when it boils at 5°F, which is why it is such a strong refrigerant.

      Why is ammonia called R-717?

      Under ASHRAE Standard 34, inorganic refrigerants are numbered 700 plus their molecular mass. Ammonia's molecular mass is about 17, so it is R-717. Water is R-718 and carbon dioxide is R-744 for the same reason.

      What is the biggest danger of an ammonia leak?

      Toxicity. Ammonia can burn in a narrow range, but the immediate danger to people is breathing it. The IDLH (Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health) level is 300 ppm.

      Why can't you use copper or brass with ammonia?

      Ammonia attacks copper, brass and other copper alloys, especially with moisture present. It will eat through parts and cause failures. Ammonia systems use steel and stainless steel, and aluminum is common in evaporator coils.

      What is “slugging” and why is it so bad for a compressor?

      Slugging is liquid getting into the compressor. Liquid can't be compressed, so it causes huge forces that can break valves, rods or rotors and destroy the compressor. That is why accumulators and recirculators separate liquid before the suction line.

      Why doesn't liquid ammonia boil away in a tank on a hot day?

      Because boiling point depends on pressure. Ammonia boils at −28°F only at atmospheric pressure (0 psig). In a sealed receiver the pressure is much higher. On an 85°F day the receiver sits at about 151.7 psig, and at that pressure ammonia boils at 85°F. It is saturated — ready to boil — but it won't boil unless it gains more heat or the pressure drops.

      What is the simple difference between superheat and subcooling?

      Superheat is heat added to a vapor after it has finished boiling. Subcooling is heat removed from a liquid after it has finished condensing. Both are measured in degrees away from the saturation temperature.

      Why do large systems use two-stage compression?

      To save energy and keep discharge temperatures down. When the gap between evaporating and condensing pressure is very large — a −40°F freezer, for example — one step of compression is inefficient and runs very hot. Two steps, with an intercooler between them, take much less total energy.

      What is a “ton of refrigeration”?

      A unit of cooling capacity: the heat needed to melt one ton (2,000 lb) of ice in 24 hours. It equals 12,000 BTU per hour, or 200 BTU per minute.

      What does a high-pressure receiver do?

      It stores the liquid that drains from the condenser. It keeps a steady supply of liquid for the expansion valves and gives the charge somewhere to go as loads, defrosts and seasons change.

      What is the main advantage of liquid recirculation?

      Strong heat transfer. Pumping 2 to 4 times more liquid than boils keeps the whole coil surface wet, so the coils do more work. That allows higher suction pressure for the same room temperature, which saves energy. One central vessel can also serve many evaporators.

      Is ammonia flammable?

      Yes, but it is hard to ignite. It needs a concentration between about 15% and 28% in air and a strong ignition source. Toxicity is the more immediate hazard in most leaks.

      What are non-condensables and why are they bad?

      Gases such as air that get into the system and do not condense. They collect in the condenser, take up surface and raise the condensing pressure above what the liquid temperature says it should be. The compressor works harder and uses more energy. An automatic purger removes them.

      Why does frost or ice form on a suction line?

      Any surface below 32°F collects frost from moisture in the air. Low-temperature suction lines are below freezing, so they must be insulated with a sealed vapor retarder. Frost on insulated pipe means the insulation or vapor retarder is damaged. Sudden heavy frost at a compressor that normally runs dry can also mean liquid is coming back — a slugging risk.

      What is the difference between flooded and DX (direct expansion)?

      A flooded evaporator stays full of liquid, with a surge drum managing the level. A DX evaporator gets just enough liquid to boil off completely and uses the last part of the coil to superheat the vapor. Flooded transfers heat better but holds more charge; DX is simpler and holds less.

      What does an accumulator (suction trap) do?

      It is the compressor's last line of defense. It is a vessel in the suction line that catches any liquid carried over from the evaporators before it can reach the compressors.

      Why must oil be drained from an ammonia system?

      Ammonia oil does not mix with ammonia, and it is heavier. The oil that gets past the oil separators does not return to the compressor with the refrigerant. It sinks and collects in low-side vessels — recirculators, surge drums and accumulators — and at low points. Left there, it fouls heat transfer surfaces and can find its way into evaporators. It is drained from oil pots on those vessels using a safe procedure.

      What is a thermosyphon oil cooler?

      A passive way to cool screw-compressor oil. Liquid ammonia flows by gravity from a thermosyphon receiver down to the oil cooler. Hot oil makes some of it boil, and the lighter vapor-liquid mix rises back to the receiver. The vapor goes on to the condenser. No pump is needed.

      What does “pulling a vacuum” on a system mean?

      Using a vacuum pump to remove air and moisture from piping or a vessel before ammonia goes in. It keeps non-condensables and water out of the system.

      What is the real purpose of an intercooler?

      To make the second stage of compression easier. It cools the hot gas from the booster compressor, so the high-stage compressor takes in cooler, denser gas and uses less power. Most intercoolers also subcool the liquid going to the low-temperature evaporators.

      If a relief valve opens, can it go back in service?

      Replace it. After a relief valve has discharged it may not reseat tightly and may leak or lift at the wrong pressure. Treat the event as a leak, find the cause, and follow your site procedure and the valve maker's instructions.
