The Vapor-Compression Refrigeration Cycle

Fundamental Principle

Industrial refrigeration is a process of moving thermal energy from where it's not wanted to where it's less objectionable. This is accomplished through the continuous, closed-loop vapor-compression cycle that relies on phase changes of the refrigerant.

1. Evaporation (Heat Absorption)

Location: Evaporator coils in cold rooms, or dedicated heat exchangers like a Plate and Frame Chiller Package.

Process: Low-pressure liquid/vapor mixture absorbs heat from surroundings, causing liquid to boil and change to vapor at nearly constant temperature and pressure.

Key Point: Ammonia's high latent heat of vaporization (565 BTU/lb at 5°F) means less refrigerant mass needed for same cooling effect.

Refrigeration Effect = h₁ - h₄
(Enthalpy leaving evaporator - Enthalpy entering evaporator)

After Compression: Oil Separation

Immediately after the compressor, the hot, high-pressure discharge gas, which contains entrained oil, passes through an Oil Separator (e.g., a Mycom Oil Separator). This vessel removes the majority of the lubricating oil from the ammonia vapor, returning it to the compressor's lubrication system. Efficient oil separation is critical for maintaining condenser efficiency and preventing oil from fouling the low-side of the system.

🔑 Key Thermodynamic Concepts

Superheat

Heat added to vapor after complete evaporation. Ensures no liquid enters compressor (prevents slugging damage).

Superheat = Tactual - Tsaturation

Subcooling

Heat removed from liquid after complete condensation. Increases system efficiency by ensuring more liquid reaches expansion valve.

Subcooling = Tsaturation - Tactual

Fun Facts & The Universal Cycle

Interesting Ammonia Facts

  • Ammonia's distinct smell allows leaks to be detected by humans at concentrations far below dangerous levels.
  • Because ammonia is lighter than air, it will rise and dissipate in well-ventilated areas, a key safety feature.
  • Unlike synthetic refrigerants, ammonia is a naturally occurring compound in the environment.

The Same Cycle, Different Scales

The four-step cycle you're learning is universal. From a massive food processing plant to the fridge in your kitchen, the principle is identical—only the scale, components, and refrigerant change.

Industrial Ammonia System

Evaporation happens in huge air units in a freezer warehouse or a plate chiller cooling glycol. Compression is done by powerful screw compressors. Condensation occurs in large evaporative condensers on the roof. Expansion is controlled by precise industrial valves.

Residential HVAC / Heat Pump

Evaporation happens in the indoor coil (the "A-coil") in your air handler, absorbing heat from your house. Compression occurs in the outdoor unit. Condensation happens in the same outdoor unit's coil, rejecting heat to the outside air. Expansion is done by a small TXV or orifice. A heat pump simply uses a reversing valve to swap the function of the indoor and outdoor coils.

Kitchen Refrigerator

Evaporation takes place in the small coils inside the freezer section. Compression is handled by the small, black, sealed compressor on the back. Condensation occurs in the coils on the back of the fridge (or sometimes underneath with a fan), releasing heat into your kitchen. Expansion is managed by a simple, tiny capillary tube instead of a mechanical valve.

Pressure-Enthalpy (P-h) Diagram

The Engineer's Roadmap

The P-h diagram is the most powerful tool for visualizing the refrigeration cycle. It's a map that tells the story of the refrigerant's state—its pressure, its energy (enthalpy), and whether it's a liquid, vapor, or a mix—at every point in the system. Mastering this diagram is key to understanding system performance and troubleshooting problems.

Ammonia (R-717) P-h Diagram

Decoding the Diagram: The Three States of Ammonia

The diagram is divided into three distinct regions, representing the physical state of the ammonia.

💧 Subcooled Liquid Region (Left)

Here, ammonia is a stable, high-pressure liquid, cooled below its boiling point. It holds all its cooling potential, ready to be released.

💨 Two-Phase Dome (Center)

This is where the magic happens. Inside the dome, ammonia exists as a chaotic, boiling mixture of liquid and vapor. This is the only state where it can absorb or reject large amounts of heat without changing temperature.

🔥 Superheated Vapor Region (Right)

In this state, ammonia is a hot, low-density gas, heated above its boiling point. It has finished its cooling job and is now full of heat energy that needs to be removed from the system.

A Refrigerant's Journey: Mapping Components to the Cycle

Let's trace the path of ammonia through the system, watching how its state changes on the P-h diagram and seeing which component is responsible for each step.

🌬️

Evaporation: The Heat Sponge

In the Evaporator, the refrigerant soaks up heat, boiling from a liquid/vapor mix into a pure vapor. This is the long horizontal line at the bottom of the diagram, showing a large increase in energy (enthalpy).

⚙️

Compression: The Big Squeeze

The Compressor takes the vapor and adds work, skyrocketing its pressure and temperature. An Accumulator before the compressor ensures no liquid enters. On the diagram, this is the sharp upward and rightward path.

💧

Condensation: The Cool Down

The hot gas enters the Condenser and rejects its heat, turning back into a liquid. This liquid is stored in the High Pressure Receiver. This is the long horizontal line at the top of the diagram, showing a large decrease in energy.

⚡

Expansion: The Pressure Plunge

The high-pressure liquid passes through the Expansion Valve, where its pressure drops instantly. This throttling process is a straight vertical line down on the diagram, returning the refrigerant to its cold, low-pressure state.

Advanced Cycle Modifications

More complex components like Economizers and Intercoolers alter the basic shape of the P-h diagram to improve efficiency.

  • An Economizer port on a screw compressor creates an intermediate step on the compression line. It takes some of the flash gas created during expansion and injects it partway through the compression process. This improves efficiency by subcooling the main liquid flow, increasing the refrigeration effect.
  • An Intercooler in a two-stage system creates an entire intermediate pressure loop on the diagram. It sits between the low and high pressure levels, desuperheating the gas from the first stage of compression before it enters the second, which saves a significant amount of energy.

Liquid Recirculation (Overfeed) System Flow

Understanding the Overfeed Ratio (3:1)

The diagram illustrates a key advantage of liquid recirculation systems: the overfeed ratio. This means much more liquid refrigerant is pumped to the evaporator than is actually needed for the cooling load.

  • For a 3:1 ratio: For every 3 pounds of liquid ammonia sent to the evaporator...
  • 1 pound evaporates (boils), absorbing heat and providing the cooling effect.
  • 2 pounds remain as liquid and are carried back to the recirculator along with the vapor. This is the "wet return."

This process ensures the entire inner surface of the evaporator coil is wetted, maximizing heat transfer and efficiency. The recirculator vessel then separates the liquid (to be pumped again) from the pure "dry" vapor, which is safely sent back to the compressor.

System Architectures

Three Fundamental System Types

The method of feeding liquid to evaporators defines the system's fundamental architecture, each with distinct advantages and applications.

🔹 Direct Expansion (DX)

Method: Thermostatic expansion valve meters exact amount of liquid for complete evaporation with slight superheat.

Advantages: Simple design, Lower refrigerant charge, Less expensive initially, Easier control

Disadvantages: Less efficient heat transfer, Final coil portion used for superheating, Limited to smaller applications

Typical Component Cycle:

  1. Compressor
  2. Oil Separator
  3. Condenser
  4. High Pressure Receiver
  5. Thermostatic Expansion Valve (TXV)
  6. Evaporator (e.g. Air Cooled Unit)
  7. Accumulator (Suction Trap)
  8. Back to Compressor

🔹 Flooded Systems

Method: Evaporator heat transfer surfaces continuously wetted with liquid refrigerant via a Surge Drum and float control.

Advantages: Maximum heat transfer efficiency, Complete surface utilization, Stable operation

Disadvantages: Larger refrigerant charge, Complex oil management, Requires careful level control

Typical Component Cycle:

  1. Compressor
  2. Oil Separator
  3. Condenser
  4. High Pressure Receiver
  5. Float Expansion Valve (High or Low Side)
  6. Surge Drum
  7. Evaporator (e.g. Plate and Frame Chiller)
  8. Back to Surge Drum (Wet Return)
  9. Back to Compressor (Dry Suction from Surge Drum)

🔹 Liquid Recirculation (Overfeed)

Method: A Recirculator Package with mechanical pumps circulates 2-4 times the evaporation amount from a central vessel.

Advantages: Highest efficiency, Excellent oil return, Simplified multi-evaporator control, Most common in large plants

Disadvantages: Higher initial cost, More complex pumping systems, Larger refrigerant inventory

Typical Component Cycle:

  1. Compressor
  2. Oil Separator (may have a Thermosyphon Receiver for cooling)
  3. Condenser
  4. High Pressure or Controlled Pressure Receiver
  5. Liquid Makeup Station (feeds recirculator)
  6. Recirculator Package (with Pumps)
  7. Multiple Evaporators
  8. Back to Recirculator Package (Wet Return)
  9. Back to Compressor (Dry Suction from Recirculator)
  10. Note: An Intercooler would be used in a 2-stage version of this system.

🛠️ Critical Safety Procedure: Oil Pot Draining

Why Critical: Oil accumulates in system and must be safely removed to prevent compressor damage and maintain efficiency.

Step-by-Step Procedure:

  1. PPE: Don face shield, chemical goggles, and gloves
  2. Isolate: Close inlet and outlet valves to oil pot
  3. Equalize: Open equalize valve to reduce pressure
  4. Vent: Carefully vent remaining pressure
  5. Drain: Slowly open drain valve to collect oil
  6. Purge: Clear lines with nitrogen if required
  7. Return: Close drain, open equalize, then isolation valves
  8. Document: Record oil quantity and condition

Emergency Response

In case of ammonia leak:

  • Evacuate area immediately
  • Alert emergency response team
  • Shut off electrical equipment if safe to do so
  • Isolate refrigerant sources
  • Apply water spray to reduce vapor concentration
  • Never enter leak area without proper PPE and SCBA

Refrigeration Calculations & Sizing

Step 1: Defining the Job - The "Ton of Refrigeration"

Everything starts with knowing how much cooling you need to do. The universal unit for this is the Ton of Refrigeration (TR). Historically, it's based on the energy needed to melt one ton (2,000 lbs) of ice in 24 hours. For our purposes, the key takeaway is its value in modern units:

1 Ton of Refrigeration (TR) = 200 BTU/min

This number is the foundation. If a client needs 500 TR of cooling, we know we need to design a system capable of removing 100,000 BTU of heat every single minute.

Step 2: From Work to Weight - Calculating Mass Flow Rate

Now that we know the energy to be removed, we need to figure out how much refrigerant we need to circulate. This is the Mass Flow Rate (in pounds per minute). This depends entirely on the refrigerant's "superpower": its latent heat of vaporization. This is the amount of energy one pound of the liquid can absorb when it boils into a vapor.

Ammonia is an excellent refrigerant because it has a very high latent heat. It can absorb a lot of energy per pound. Other refrigerants, like CO2, have a much lower latent heat, meaning you need to circulate many more pounds of it to achieve the same amount of cooling.

Step 3: From Weight to Space - Calculating Volumetric Flow

Pipes, pumps, and vessels don't care about weight; they care about the volume of fluid passing through them. So, the next step is to convert our mass flow (lbs/min) into Volumetric Flow.

  • For liquids, this is expressed in Gallons Per Minute (GPM).
  • For vapor, this is expressed in Actual Cubic Feet per Minute (ACFM).

This is where the design starts to become physical. The calculated GPM tells us what size pump we need, and the ACFM will help us determine how large our vessels and suction pipes must be.

Step 4: The Efficiency Multiplier - The Overfeed Ratio

In many large systems, we use a technique called Liquid Overfeed. Instead of sending just enough liquid to the evaporator to boil off, we send much more—typically 3 to 4 times the amount. This is the Overfeed Ratio.

Why do this? It guarantees the entire inside surface of the evaporator is always drenched in liquid ammonia, which makes the heat transfer incredibly efficient. This decision, however, directly multiplies the required GPM, meaning we need a much larger pump. It's a trade-off between higher equipment cost and better long-term performance.

Step 5: Sizing for Safety - Vapor-Liquid Separation

This is one of the most critical steps. In vessels like Recirculators and Accumulators, we need the liquid droplets to fall out of the vapor stream before the vapor heads back to the compressor. If liquid gets into the compressor, it can cause catastrophic failure.

To prevent this, we calculate a "speed limit" for the vapor inside the vessel, known as the Maximum Separation Velocity. This is determined by the Souders-Brown equation, which balances gravity's pull on the liquid droplets against the drag force of the rising vapor. The vessel's diameter is then chosen to be large enough so that the Actual Vapor Velocity (the ACFM divided by the vessel's cross-sectional area) is kept well below this speed limit, ensuring safe separation.

System Troubleshooting

🔍 Diagnostic Approach

Effective troubleshooting requires systematic analysis of symptoms, followed by targeted testing and verification. Use the search bar below to find common issues, or browse the list. Always consult the P-h diagram as your most powerful diagnostic tool.

High Discharge / Condensing Pressure

Symptoms

  • Elevated compressor power consumption.
  • Reduced system cooling capacity.
  • Compressor may trip on high-pressure safety cutout.

Possible Causes

  • Dirty condenser coils (air-cooled) or fouled tubes/plates (evaporative/water-cooled).
  • Failed condenser fan or pump.
  • Presence of non-condensable gases (air) in the system.
  • Insufficient airflow or water flow to the condenser.
  • System is overcharged with refrigerant.

Solutions

  • Clean condenser surfaces according to manufacturer guidelines.
  • Inspect and repair/replace failed fans or pumps.
  • Perform a non-condensable gas purge from the top of the condenser or receiver.
  • Check for and clear any obstructions to condenser air/water flow.
  • Safely recover excess refrigerant to the proper level.

Low Suction / Evaporating Pressure

Symptoms

  • Reduced cooling capacity in the refrigerated space.
  • Compressor may short-cycle on the low-pressure cutout.
  • Potential for evaporator coils to freeze up.

Possible Causes

  • Insufficient cooling load on the evaporator.
  • Blocked or clogged strainers in the liquid or suction line.
  • Evaporator coils are heavily frosted or iced over, blocking airflow.
  • Liquid line solenoid or expansion valve is malfunctioning or closed.
  • System is undercharged with refrigerant.

Solutions

  • Verify the load; a lightly loaded room will naturally have lower pressure.
  • Isolate, pump down, and clean all relevant strainers.
  • Initiate a defrost cycle and verify its proper operation.
  • Inspect, repair, or replace faulty control valves.
  • Find and repair any leaks, then charge the system to the correct level.

Liquid Carryover (Slugging) to Compressor

Symptoms

  • Loud, rhythmic knocking or banging noise from the compressor.
  • Heavy frost or ice on the compressor suction line or cylinder heads.
  • Erratic oil pressure and potential for compressor to trip on oil safety.
  • CRITICAL: This can cause immediate, catastrophic compressor failure.

Possible Causes

  • Expansion valve failed open or is oversized, flooding the evaporator.
  • High liquid level in the suction accumulator or recirculator vessel.
  • Rapid load change causing a surge of liquid back to the compressor.
  • Improperly set up or failed hot gas defrost system.

Solutions

  • IMMEDIATELY SHUT DOWN THE AFFECTED COMPRESSOR.
  • Manually close the liquid feed to the problem evaporator.
  • Identify the source of the liquid (e.g., check vessel levels, inspect expansion valves).
  • Once the cause is found and fixed, carefully restart the system and monitor suction conditions closely.

High Discharge Temperature

Symptoms

  • Discharge temperature exceeds 250-300°F (121-149°C).
  • Oil may darken or smell burnt (oil breakdown).
  • Reduced system efficiency and potential for compressor damage.

Possible Causes

  • High compression ratio (very low suction pressure combined with high discharge pressure).
  • Excessive superheat at the compressor suction.
  • Insufficient cylinder cooling (on reciprocating compressors).
  • Worn compressor valves or rings.

Solutions

  • Address the causes of low suction or high discharge pressure first.
  • Check superheat settings; ensure it's not excessively high (typically 10-20°F is desired).
  • Check compressor cooling systems.
  • If other causes are ruled out, an internal compressor inspection may be needed.

High Power Consumption

Symptoms

  • Elevated energy bills without a corresponding increase in cooling load.
  • Compressor motor running at higher-than-normal amperage.

Possible Causes

  • High condensing pressure (dirty condenser, non-condensables).
  • Low evaporating pressure (forcing a higher compression ratio).
  • Inefficient compressor operation (worn parts).
  • Poor oil management, fouling heat exchangers.

Solutions

  • Perform a full system check, focusing on maintaining design pressures.
  • Clean all heat exchange surfaces (condensers and evaporators).
  • Purge non-condensables.
  • Ensure proper oil levels and perform regular oil changes.

Pump Pressure & Rotation Issues

Symptoms

  • Low or no differential pressure across the pump.
  • Excessively high differential pressure (dead-heading).
  • High motor amps with low flow.
  • Excessive noise or vibration from the pump.

Possible Causes

  • Incorrect Rotation (Low Pressure): Pump is wired to run backwards.
  • Bypass Valve Open (Low Pressure): A relief or bypass valve is stuck open.
  • Closed Discharge Valve (High Pressure): A downstream valve is closed.

Solutions

  • For Incorrect Rotation: Safely LOTO the pump. Swap any two of the three power leads on a 3-phase motor.
  • For Open Bypass: Verify and close all bypass and relief lines.
  • For Closed Discharge: Immediately shut down the pump. Verify all required discharge valves are open before restarting.

Poor Oil Return

Symptoms

  • Low oil level in the compressor sump or oil separator.
  • Compressor trips on low oil safety.
  • Reduced efficiency in evaporators due to oil fouling.

Possible Causes

  • Improper pipe sizing or slope in suction lines.
  • Refrigerant velocity is too low to carry oil back.
  • Oil separator is malfunctioning or needs draining.
  • System is low on refrigerant, affecting velocities.

Solutions

  • Verify piping is installed according to IIAR standards.
  • Check system charge and operating parameters to ensure proper velocities.
  • Inspect, test, and maintain the oil separator and oil management system.
  • Manually drain oil from low points in the system (e.g., oil pots) following safe procedures.

Presence of Non-Condensables (Air)

Symptoms

  • Condensing pressure is significantly higher than the pressure corresponding to the refrigerant's saturation temperature.
  • High compressor power draw.
  • Bubbles visible in the liquid line sight glass (if far from the expansion valve).

Possible Causes

  • Air leaked into the system, especially on the low-pressure side when operating in a vacuum.
  • Improper evacuation procedures after maintenance.

Solutions

  • Use an auto-purger to continuously remove non-condensables.
  • Manually purge air from the highest point of the high-pressure side (e.g., top of the condenser).
  • Find and repair the source of the leak.

Refrigerant Pump Cavitation

Symptoms

  • Loud, rattling or gravel-like noise from the pump.
  • Fluctuating discharge pressure and flow.
  • Premature wear of pump seals and bearings.

Possible Causes

  • Insufficient Net Positive Suction Head (NPSH) - liquid level in the supply vessel is too low.
  • Refrigerant is too close to its saturation temperature (not enough subcooling).
  • Clogged suction strainer.

Solutions

  • Ensure proper liquid level in the recirculator or surge drum.
  • Check for sources of heat gain in the suction line; insulate if necessary.
  • Isolate, pump down, and clean the pump's suction strainer.

Evaporator Coil Icing

Symptoms

  • A solid block of ice forms on the evaporator coil.
  • Severely restricted or blocked airflow.
  • Insufficient cooling and rising space temperatures.

Possible Causes

  • Defrost cycle is failing (timer, heaters, hot gas solenoids).
  • Excessive moisture infiltrating the refrigerated space (e.g., doors left open, poor seals).
  • Fans not turning off during defrost.

Solutions

  • Thoroughly troubleshoot the defrost system: check timers, contactors, heaters, and valves.
  • Inspect and repair all door seals and minimize traffic into the space.
  • Verify that evaporator fans are de-energizing during the defrost cycle.

Solenoid Valve Failure

Symptoms

  • Valve fails to open, causing no flow to an evaporator.
  • Valve fails to close, causing continuous flow and potential for liquid slugging.
  • Humming or buzzing from the valve coil.

Possible Causes

  • Burned-out or failed electrical coil.
  • Debris lodged inside the valve, preventing the piston or diaphragm from seating.
  • Damaged internal parts (diaphragm, piston).
  • Incorrect voltage supplied to the coil.

Solutions

  • Safely de-energize and check the coil for continuity; replace if necessary.
  • Pump down the affected section, disassemble the valve, and clean it.
  • Use a valve repair kit to replace internal parts.
  • Verify the power supply matches the coil's voltage rating.

Pressure Relief Valve Venting

Symptoms

  • Audible sound of gas escaping.
  • Loss of refrigerant from the system.
  • Ammonia detectors may alarm.

Possible Causes

  • Overpressure from an external heat source, such as a fire.
  • Hydraulic expansion: liquid refrigerant trapped between two closed valves expands as it warms.
  • System over-pressurization from a malfunctioning compressor or control failure.
  • Faulty or weeping relief valve.

Solutions

  • EVACUATE AND ADDRESS AS AN ACTIVE LEAK.
  • Identify and correct the cause of overpressure immediately.
  • Never block the discharge of a relief valve.
  • Relief valves must be replaced after any venting event, as they may not reseat properly.

Compressor Short-Cycling

Symptoms

  • Compressor starts and stops in rapid succession (e.g., more than 6-8 times per hour).
  • Causes excessive wear on motor starters and the compressor.

Possible Causes

  • Low-pressure or high-pressure control setpoints are too close together (narrow differential).
  • Oversized compressor for the current cooling load.
  • Faulty or improperly located control sensors.
  • Rapid changes in system load.

Solutions

  • Widen the differential pressure settings on the control cutouts.
  • If oversized, consider installing capacity control or a smaller compressor.
  • Check calibration and placement of all pressure and temperature sensors.
  • Investigate and stabilize system load fluctuations where possible.

Chiller Freeze-Up

Symptoms

  • Process fluid (e.g., glycol, brine, water) freezes inside the chiller.
  • Low suction pressure and temperature.
  • Potential for ruptured chiller tubes, leading to catastrophic failure and refrigerant release.

Possible Causes

  • Insufficient flow of process fluid through the chiller.
  • Low-temperature safety cutout is set too low or has failed.
  • Refrigerant control valve (e.g., expansion valve) is malfunctioning.
  • Incorrect concentration of antifreeze in the process fluid.

Solutions

  • Verify process fluid pumps are running and there are no closed valves or blockages.
  • Test and calibrate all safety cutouts to the manufacturer's specification.
  • Inspect and repair refrigerant control valves.
  • Test the freeze point of the process fluid and adjust concentration as needed.

High Suction Pressure

Symptoms

  • Increased compressor load and power consumption.
  • Higher-than-desired temperatures in the refrigerated space.
  • Reduced dehumidification in the space.

Possible Causes

  • Excessive cooling load (e.g., large amount of warm product introduced).
  • Thermostatic expansion valve (TXV) is stuck open or has a misplaced sensing bulb.
  • Leaking hot gas bypass or defrost valves.
  • Incorrect pressure regulator settings.

Solutions

  • Check system load against design capacity.
  • Inspect the TXV, ensuring the bulb is securely mounted on the suction line and insulated.
  • Inspect and repair any leaking bypass or hot gas valves.
  • Verify and adjust pressure regulator setpoints.
